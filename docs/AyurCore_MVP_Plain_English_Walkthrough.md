# How AyurCore Works — A Plain-English Walkthrough

## What this actually does

AyurCore lets a doctor speak naturally during a consultation — in English, Malayalam, or the doctor's own natural mix of both — and turns that into a correct, safe, ready-to-dispense prescription, without the doctor ever having to type into a form or click through a list. The two things this document focuses on, because they're the two things people ask about first, are: how the voice part works, and how we make absolutely sure the patient's private information never leaves our own system while an outside AI is helping with the medical part.

## Step 1: Getting the doctor's words into the system — three ways

Doctors don't all work the same way, so this isn't built around only one method. There are three real paths, and it's worth walking through what happens on each one, because the answer to "is this safe" is slightly different depending on which one is used.

**Option one: speaking.** The doctor presses a single "record" button — on a phone, a tablet, or whatever computer is in the consultation room, nothing special needed — and just talks, the way they'd naturally describe the case to a colleague. In English, in Malayalam, or in the mixed way many people in Kerala actually speak, switching between the two mid-sentence. They press stop, and within a few seconds the spoken words appear on screen as written text.

Here's the one honest thing worth knowing about this path: for that brief moment before it's turned into text, what exists is an actual audio recording — and if the doctor happens to say the patient's name out loud while dictating, that name is, for a few seconds, sitting inside a sound clip on its way to the speech-to-text service that turns it into words. That service is a trusted partner chosen specifically because it's built for exactly this kind of Indian-language speech, but it's still worth being upfront that this one moment — turning sound into text — happens before our own name-swapping protection kicks in, because you can't hide a name inside a sentence you haven't heard yet. Once it becomes text, the very next thing that happens is the identity protection described in Step 2, no exceptions. A doctor who prefers to simply say "the patient" instead of a name while dictating removes even that brief moment entirely, and it's a small, easy habit to build in.

**Option two: typing.** The doctor types the notes directly into a text box instead of speaking — useful in a noisy room, or just personal preference. This path is actually the simplest from a privacy standpoint: there's no recording at all, at any point, so there's nothing to be careful about before Step 2. Typed words go straight into the same identity-protection step as spoken ones.

**Option three: paper.** Plenty of doctors are used to jotting a quick note on paper first, and that habit doesn't need to change. For now, paper is simply a personal aid — whatever's written on it still needs to become either spoken or typed words in the system, the same as if it had never touched paper, so it isn't a separate way in. A doctor could glance at their paper note and just read it aloud, or type it up, whichever's faster in the moment.

There is a further option worth mentioning honestly as something for later rather than now: teaching the system to read a photo of the handwritten note directly, the same way a banking app can scan a photo of a check and pull out the numbers. It's a real possibility, but two things make it a "not yet": handwriting is genuinely hard to read reliably, even for a person, let alone a computer, so it would need careful double-checking before anyone trusted it; and a photo of a handwritten note has the exact same honest caveat as the audio recording above — if the patient's name is written on the paper, it's sitting in that photo for a moment before anything can protect it. It's a good idea for a later version, not a shortcut worth rushing into now.

Whichever of the three paths gets used, the destination is identical: plain written text, and from that point on the system treats it exactly the same regardless of how it arrived. That's the reassuring part — the identity protection in the next step doesn't know or care whether the words came from a microphone, a keyboard, or a doctor reading their own handwriting out loud. It protects all of it the same way, every time.

## Step 2: Protecting the patient's identity — before anything else happens

This is the part we built first and care about most, so it's worth explaining carefully.

Here's the problem: to understand what herbs and what dosage a doctor is describing, we need help from a large, powerful AI system — the kind of AI made by companies like Google. That AI runs on computers outside our own building, out on the internet. We do **not** want a patient's real name, phone number, or any other identifying detail ever leaving our system and reaching that outside AI, even though the AI itself is trustworthy — because the whole point of protecting a patient's privacy is not needing to trust every single computer their information might pass through.

So before the doctor's words go anywhere near that outside AI, our own local system does something simple: it finds the patient's name (and phone number, if mentioned) wherever it appears in the notes, and swaps it out for a random, meaningless code — something like a coat-check ticket. Think of it exactly like a coat check at a busy restaurant: you hand over your coat, you get a ticket with a random number on it, and that number means nothing to anyone except the person at the counter who knows which coat it belongs to. The doctor's notes get the same treatment — every mention of "Rahul Varma" becomes something like "Patient #8F3A91" everywhere it appears, consistently, throughout the notes.

Only our own local system keeps track of which code belongs to which real patient. That information never gets written down anywhere the outside AI can see, and it's thrown away the moment that one request is finished — it isn't stored, saved, or reused later.

Right before those anonymized notes are actually sent out, our system does one more check — a final safety look-over of the exact text about to be sent, specifically searching for the patient's real name or phone number one more time. If it finds even a trace of it, still there by some mistake, it refuses to send anything at all and stops the whole process on the spot, rather than sending it anyway and hoping for the best. Nothing reaches the outside AI unless that check passes clean.

So by the time anything leaves our building, the outside AI only ever sees something like: *"Patient #8F3A91 has fatigue and poor sleep, Vata imbalance, prescribe 20 grams Ashwagandha."* It never learns who that patient actually is.

## Step 3: How the AI actually works, in plain terms

The AI we're using — Google's Gemini — isn't a doctor, and it isn't making medical decisions. It's best understood as an extremely capable reading-and-organizing assistant. It has been trained on enormous amounts of text and has become very good at one specific skill: taking messy, natural human language and turning it into clean, organized information.

So when it receives the anonymized notes, its only job is to read them and pull out two things: what imbalance the patient has (in Ayurvedic terms, a dosha imbalance — Vata, Pitta, Kapha, or a combination), and exactly which herbs were prescribed, in exactly what quantity. It hands that back as a clean, structured little summary — nothing more, nothing less. It never sees who the patient is, and it never decides anything about what to actually do — it only organizes what the doctor already said.

## Step 4: The AI double-checks itself before answering

Rather than just guessing at a herb name and hoping it's right, the AI is given a way to check its own work before it finalizes an answer — it can look up whether a herb it's about to suggest is actually one the clinic keeps in stock, and how much of it is currently on the shelf. If it's about to suggest something that isn't stocked, it finds that out immediately, before ever telling the doctor, the same way a careful assistant would double-check a fact before repeating it rather than repeating it confidently and being wrong.

## Step 5: The doctor always has the final word

This next part is the most important safety principle in the whole system: nothing the AI produces is ever automatically finalized. Once it's done reading the notes, the doctor sees exactly what it understood — the imbalance, the herbs, the quantities — laid out plainly, before anything happens to the patient's record or the clinic's stock. If it got something slightly wrong — misheard a quantity, misunderstood a phrase — the doctor corrects it right there. Only once the doctor actively confirms does anything become final. The AI's role stops at "here's what I understood" — it is never the one who decides.

## Step 6: Updating the shelf and the record

Once the doctor confirms, the system does two things at the same time, safely: it reduces the clinic's herb inventory by exactly the amount prescribed, and it writes the finished prescription into the patient's record under their real name — the name the doctor actually typed in when they started, not anything the outside AI ever saw. If, for any reason, the clinic doesn't have enough of a herb in stock, nothing gets deducted at all and the doctor is told immediately, rather than the system quietly running the clinic out of stock partway through.

## Step 7: The patient gets their care note

Right after the prescription is finalized, a short message goes out to the patient's phone over WhatsApp — a simple summary of what was prescribed and any diet or lifestyle notes, in their own language, so they walk away with a clear written reminder instead of relying on memory alone.

## The one idea that runs through all of it

The AI's entire job, from start to finish, is to listen and organize — never to decide, and never to know who the patient actually is. Every step that actually changes something — updating stock, finalizing a prescription, sending a message — happens only after a real person has looked at it and said yes. That's the whole design, in one sentence: the AI helps the doctor go faster, but the doctor, and only the doctor, is still the one in charge.

## Technical appendix: how each step actually works under the hood

Everything above is the honest picture in plain language. This section is the same seven steps again, but at the level of what the code actually does — useful for anyone technical who wants to verify none of the plain-language claims are hand-waving. A note on status: Steps 2, 3, and 6 are built and tested today; Steps 1 (voice), 4 (self-checking), 5 (the preview/confirm split), and 7 (WhatsApp) are the current build sprint, not yet live.

**Step 1 — getting words into the system.** For typed notes, the text comes straight from a form field — no external service touches it at all. For spoken notes, the recorded audio is sent to Sarvam AI's speech-to-text model (Saaras v3), which was specifically chosen because it's trained on code-mixed Indian-language speech rather than single-language dictation; the response is a plain transcript string. Paper isn't a code path — whatever's on paper has to become one of the two inputs above before the system ever sees it.

**Step 2 — protecting identity (the tokenizer).** This is a function called `tokenize_payload`. It's given the note text plus a list of the real values to protect — the patient's name, phone number, and ID, plus the individual words of the name (so "John" alone is caught the same as "John Mathew," handled by a helper called `expand_name_variants`). It sorts these longest-first, then for each one runs a case-insensitive find-and-replace across the text, substituting every occurrence with a token shaped like `[[PAT_8F3A91C2]]` — the random part generated by Python's `secrets` module, the same cryptographically secure randomness used for things like session keys, not a simple counter, so a token can't be guessed or reverse-engineered from anything about the patient. The mapping of token-to-real-value lives only in that one function call's memory — it's a plain in-memory dictionary, never written to disk, and it disappears the moment the request finishes. Immediately before anything is sent onward, a second function, `assert_no_pii_leak`, re-scans the now-tokenized text for the real name, phone, and ID one more time; if it finds any trace, it raises an error (`PIIViolationError`) and the calling code is written to stop dead at that point — the network call to Gemini simply never executes.

**Step 3 — how the model works.** The tokenized text goes to Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash model via the official `google-genai` Python library, in a single call to `generate_content`. Critically, that call includes a `response_schema` parameter pointing at a strict data definition (a Pydantic model called `ClinicalParse`, with exactly two fields: `dosha_imbalance` and `prescribed_herbs`) — this forces Gemini's output to come back as valid, predictable JSON matching that exact shape, rather than free-form text the code would have to guess at parsing.

**Step 4 — the AI checking itself.** This is done through what's called tool calling or function calling: Gemini is given a small set of real Python functions it's allowed to invoke mid-answer — for example, one that looks up whether a herb name is recognized, and one that checks current stock. When Gemini wants to use one, the API response comes back asking for that function call instead of a final answer; the code runs the real function against the real database, sends the result back to Gemini, and this can repeat briefly until Gemini is confident enough to give a final structured answer. The herb list and stock levels it's checking against are always the real, current numbers — never something the model guesses at.

**Step 5 — the doctor confirms.** This is built as two separate, distinct operations rather than one. The first (`/prescribe/preview`) runs everything from tokenizing through getting Gemini's answer and restoring the real values, and stops there — nothing about inventory or the patient record is touched yet. Restoring real values is handled by `rehydrate_payload`, which walks through every part of Gemini's response, including anything nested, and swaps each token back for its real value using the same mapping from Step 2; a second check, `find_unresolved_tokens`, scans the result once more afterward specifically looking for any token-shaped text that didn't get restored, as a safety net against something slipping through unnoticed. Only the second operation (`/prescribe/confirm`), triggered by the doctor's explicit approval, goes on to touch the database — and it makes no AI call of any kind. From that point on, everything is ordinary, predictable code, not a model's judgment.

**Step 6 — updating stock and the record.** This runs inside a single database transaction opened with `BEGIN IMMEDIATE`, a SQLite instruction that locks the database for writing right away so two doctors' requests can't collide and corrupt the count. Each herb is deducted with a conditional update — subtract the amount, but only if there's currently enough in stock — and the code checks whether that update actually changed anything. If any single herb didn't have enough stock, the entire transaction is rolled back, undoing every change made so far in that same request, and an error is returned instead — so a prescription either fully succeeds or leaves no trace of a partial attempt, never something in between.

**Step 7 — the WhatsApp message.** Once the database transaction above has actually committed successfully, a short message is built from the now-real, confirmed prescription details and sent through a WhatsApp Business API provider to the phone number the doctor entered directly into the system at the start — a number that, like the patient's name, was never part of what was sent to Gemini in Step 3.
